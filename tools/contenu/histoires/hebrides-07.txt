Duncan Fraser read Jens's report twice. Then he called a meeting in the portable office. On a screen on the wall, there was a man from the blade manufacturer in Germany, Mr Kessler.

"I've seen Mr Madsen's images," said Mr Kessler politely. "The defects are real, but they're small. In our opinion, they're within tolerance."

"Within tolerance?" repeated Jens. "Forty centimetres at the root?"

"Our engineers have calculated the risk. With sensors inside the blades to monitor any change, the blades can operate safely. If the defect grows, the sensors will warn us, and we'll replace the blades."

"And how long to replace them now?" asked Duncan.

Mr Kessler looked at his papers. "Six months. Minimum."

Duncan looked at the calendar on the wall. The red circle around the thirty-first of December.

"Six months," he said slowly. "That means we miss the grid deadline. We lose the government price guarantee. The investors leave. The project dies. Two hundred workers lose their jobs. The community loses its million pounds a year." He turned to Jens. "Mr Madsen, can you guarantee that these blades will break?"

"Nobody can guarantee that a blade will break. I can tell you that they're damaged and that the damage will grow."

"Can you guarantee that they'll break before the sensors warn us?"

Jens was silent for a moment. "No."

"Then we install them," said Duncan. "With sensors. On one turbine, so we can watch them closely. And we replace them in the summer, when the new ones arrive."

"I won't sign the inspection certificate," said Jens.

"That's your right," said Duncan. "The manufacturer will sign it."

Jens closed his notebook and looked at Hugo. Hugo looked at the floor.

He knew he should say something. But Duncan's arguments were not stupid. Two hundred jobs. A million pounds a year for the island.

"Which turbine?" Hugo heard himself ask.

Duncan looked at the map on the wall. "Number seven. It's already built."

Hugo looked at the map too.

Turbine seven was the one closest to the road. The road with the yellow school bus.

Duncan Fraser a lu le rapport de Jens deux fois. Puis il a organisé une réunion dans le bureau préfabriqué. Sur un écran au mur, il y avait un homme du fabricant des pales en Allemagne, M. Kessler.

« J'ai vu les images de M. Madsen, a dit M. Kessler poliment. Les défauts sont réels, mais ils sont petits. À notre avis, ils restent dans les tolérances.

— Dans les tolérances ? a répété Jens. Quarante centimètres à la base ?

— Nos ingénieurs ont calculé le risque. Avec des capteurs à l'intérieur des pales pour surveiller toute évolution, les pales peuvent fonctionner en toute sécurité. Si le défaut s'agrandit, les capteurs nous préviendront, et nous remplacerons les pales.

— Et combien de temps pour les remplacer maintenant ? » a demandé Duncan.

M. Kessler a regardé ses papiers. « Six mois. Au minimum. »

Duncan a regardé le calendrier au mur. Le cercle rouge autour du trente et un décembre.

« Six mois, a-t-il dit lentement. Ça veut dire qu'on rate l'échéance du réseau. On perd la garantie de prix de l'État. Les investisseurs partent. Le projet meurt.
