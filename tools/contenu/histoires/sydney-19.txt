--- EN
The following Monday, Jack asked Manon to come for a walk.

They walked through Surry Hills to a small park with big fig trees. Jack bought two coffees from a van and sat down on a bench.

"I owe you an apology," he said finally.

Manon looked at him, surprised.

"I shouted at you. In front of everyone. I said you didn't understand advertising." He turned his coffee cup in his hands. "You understood it better than I did. I've been doing this job for twenty-five years, and I'd forgotten what it's for."

"What is it for?"

Jack thought about it.

"When I started, I thought advertising was about telling stories that make people feel something. Then, slowly, without noticing, it became about telling the stories that clients want to pay for." He laughed softly. "Those aren't always the same thing."

"There's something else," said Jack. "My daughter, Ruby. She's sixteen. Every Friday, she goes on strike from school. For the climate." He shook his head. "For two years, I told her she was naive. That the world doesn't work like that. That her father needed big clients to pay for her school."

"And now?"

"On Thursday night, after we lost, I told her about the pitch. About 'Not Yet.' About Kirra." He was quiet for a moment. "I showed her the film on my phone."

"What did she say?"

Jack looked at the fig trees. His eyes were wet.

"She didn't say anything. She just hugged me. For a long time. She hasn't hugged me since she was twelve." "Then she said: 'Dad, that's the first ad you've made that I'd show my friends.'"

Manon did not know what to say. So she said nothing.

After a while, Jack stood up.

"Come on. Back to work." He smiled for the first time in a week. "I've decided something. That slogan on the wall. 'No bad ideas.' I'm going to change it."

"To what?"

"I don't know yet," said Jack. "But you'll write it."
