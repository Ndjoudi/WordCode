On Monday, the whole team met in the big meeting room for the brief. A brief is the document a client gives to an agency to explain what it wants. What to sell, to whom, and what message to send.

The account director, Belinda Cross, presented it. She was about fifty, very elegant, with short silver hair and a voice that never rose. Her job was to manage the relationship with clients.

"Coralis Energy," she began. "Australia's third-biggest energy company. They produce natural gas, mostly from fields off the coast of Queensland."

She clicked to the next slide.

"Their problem: young Australians don't like them. In surveys, people under thirty-five associate Coralis with pollution, climate change and the damage to the Great Barrier Reef." She paused. "Their new chief executive, Grant Mackie, wants to change that."

Next slide. A photograph of a smiling man of about fifty-five in front of a sunset.

"Coralis has announced a new strategy. They promise to be 'carbon neutral by 2030.' That means that by 2030, they'll take as much carbon out of the air as they put into it. They want a campaign that makes Australians proud of Coralis. A campaign that says: this is a clean, green Australian company, protecting our future."

On the last slide, there were three words in large letters. The client's suggestion for the slogan.

CLEAN. GREEN. GOLD.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

"Questions?" asked Belinda.

Manon raised her hand. "How will they become carbon neutral by 2030? If they produce gas?"

Belinda smiled politely. "That's their business, Manon, not ours. They have a plan. It's in their annual report."

"Can I read it?"

"Of course. It's two hundred pages." Belinda raised an eyebrow. "Most people don't."

After the meeting, Noah came to Manon's desk.

"You asked the question everybody was thinking," he said quietly. "Nobody else would."

"Is that bad?"

"In this agency?" Noah thought about it. "It's unusual."

That evening, Manon downloaded the Coralis annual report. Two hundred and twelve pages. She made a coffee and started at page one.

La directrice de clientèle, Belinda Cross, l'a présenté. Elle avait une cinquantaine d'années, très élégante, avec des cheveux argentés courts et une voix qui ne montait jamais. Son travail était de gérer la relation avec les clients.

« Coralis Energy, a-t-elle commencé. Troisième compagnie d'énergie d'Australie. Ils produisent du gaz naturel, surtout dans des gisements au large du Queensland. »

Elle a cliqué sur la diapositive suivante.

« Leur problème : les jeunes Australiens ne les aiment pas. Dans les sondages, les moins de trente-cinq ans associent Coralis à la pollution, au changement climatique et aux dégâts sur la Grande Barrière de corail. » Elle a marqué une pause. « Leur nouveau PDG, Grant Mackie, veut changer ça. »

Diapositive suivante. La photo d'un homme souriant d'environ cinquante-cinq ans devant un coucher de soleil.

« Coralis a annoncé une nouvelle stratégie. Ils promettent d'être "neutres en carbone d'ici 2030". Ça veut dire que d'ici 2030, ils retireront de l'air autant de carbone qu'ils en émettent. Ils veulent une campagne qui rende les Australiens fiers de Coralis. Une campagne qui dise : voici une entreprise australienne propre et verte, qui protège notre avenir. »

Sur la dernière diapositive, il y avait trois mots en grosses lettres. La suggestion du client pour le slogan.
