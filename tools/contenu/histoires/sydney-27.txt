--- EN
In December, Diane Harbour announced that she was retiring. Really retiring, this time. No more Friday afternoons.

"I'm sixty-nine," she said at the morning meeting. "I've been in advertising for forty-five years. I want to spend the rest of my life swimming at Bronte, reading books and being rude to my grandchildren."

The party took place on the agency's roof terrace on a hot Friday evening. Biscuit the dog wore a small party hat and looked deeply unhappy about it.

Jack made a speech. Belinda made a speech.

At the end of the evening, Diane found Manon alone at the edge of the terrace, looking at the lights of the city.

She was carrying a heavy black case.

"For you," she said, and put it on the table.

Manon opened it. Inside was an old typewriter, black and silver, with round keys. On a small metal plate on the front, someone had engraved: D. HARBOUR, 1987.

"I wrote my first ad for this agency on it," said Diane. "And my worst one. The cigarettes. And about four thousand others in between." She touched the keys gently. "I want you to have it."

"Diane, I can't..."

"You can." Diane sat down on a chair next to her. "I was ambitious. I wanted to win. I did win. Many times." She paused. "But I forgot to ask one question. Every time. Before I wrote anything."

"What question?"

Diane looked at Manon for a long moment.

"Would I show this to my sister?" she said simply. "If I'd asked myself that question in 1992, I'd never have written that slogan." She stood up slowly. "You ask that question naturally, Manon. Most people never learn to. Don't ever stop."

She kissed Manon on the cheek and walked back towards the party.

Manon looked at the typewriter for a long time. Then she put a piece of paper in it and slowly typed one sentence, pressing each heavy key with care.

"Would I show this to my sister?"

She left the paper in the machine.

« J'ai soixante-neuf ans, a-t-elle dit à la réunion du matin. Je suis dans la publicité depuis quarante-cinq ans. Je veux passer le reste de ma vie à nager à Bronte, à lire des livres et à être désagréable avec mes petits-enfants. »

La fête a eu lieu sur la terrasse sur le toit de l'agence, par un chaud vendredi soir. Biscuit, le chien, portait un petit chapeau de fête et avait l'air profondément malheureux.

Jack a fait un discours. Belinda a fait un discours.

À la fin de la soirée, Diane a trouvé Manon seule au bord de la terrasse, à regarder les lumières de la ville.

Elle portait une lourde mallette noire.

« Pour toi », a-t-elle dit, et elle l'a posée sur la table.

Manon l'a ouverte. À l'intérieur, il y avait une vieille machine à écrire, noire et argentée, aux touches rondes. Sur une petite plaque de métal à l'avant, quelqu'un avait gravé : D. HARBOUR, 1987.

« J'ai écrit ma première pub pour cette agence dessus, a dit Diane. Et ma pire. Les cigarettes. Et environ quatre mille autres entre les deux. » Elle a touché les touches doucement. « Je veux que tu l'aies.

Diane a regardé Manon un long moment.
