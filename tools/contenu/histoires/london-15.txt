"Marc? What's wrong? It's late."

He told her everything, walking in the rain. The carrots. The tips. Abdi. The oil. Mia. Oliver's offer. One hundred and twenty thousand pounds.

His mother listened without interrupting.

When he finished, she was quiet for a long time.

"Do you remember the year the restaurant almost closed?" she asked finally. "You were twelve."

"The year of the fish."

"The year of the fish." She laughed softly. "Our fish supplier offered us a deal. Cheaper fish. Frozen, from far away. He said, 'Madame, nobody will know. Put it in the cream sauce, they'll never taste the difference.' We had no money. Your father was sick. I almost said yes."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because of Monsieur Bernard. Do you remember him? The old man who ate at table three every Friday for twenty years. He always ordered the fish. He told everybody my fish was the best in Lyon." She paused. "I imagined him eating that frozen fish and smiling at me. I couldn't do it. I would have been a liar every Friday."

Marc stopped walking. He was standing on Westminster Bridge.

"What happened?"

"We survived. Barely. Monsieur Bernard told his friends, and his friends told their friends. People came because they knew we were honest." She paused. "A restaurant can survive without money for a while, Marc. It can't survive without trust."

"Maman, it's one hundred and twenty thousand pounds."

"I know. It's a lot of money." Her voice became gentle. "But tell me something. When you look at the dishwasher, Abdi, what do you see?"

Marc thought about it. "An engineer. Someone who built bridges."

"And when you look at Oliver?"

"Someone who sells bridges that don't exist."

His mother laughed. "Then you already know your answer, my love. You called me so I could tell you that you're not crazy. You're not. Go to bed."

Marc stood on the bridge for a long time after she hung up. Then he walked home, and for the first time in a week, he slept all night.
