--- EN
Three weeks after the article, Graham called everyone to the middle of the newsroom. He was holding a piece of paper, and his face was even redder than usual.

"Numbers," he said. "Since the Little Stars story, six thousand two hundred people have taken a digital subscription to the Ledger. Six thousand two hundred. In three weeks. That's more than in the last three years."

People cheered.

"And that's not all." Graham pointed at a corner of the room. There were four large post office bags on the floor. "Letters. Real letters, on paper, with stamps. People thanking us. People telling us their stories. People asking how they can help St Anne's."

Léa went to the bags and took out a few envelopes. A pensioner in Bolton who had run every fun run with his late wife. A primary school class in Stretford that had sent drawings of the hospital with "THANK YOU LEDGER" in big coloured letters.

She found Danny reading a letter in the corner. His eyes were wet.

"From a mum," he said. "Her son was on the cancer ward last year. She slept on a plastic chair for four months." He folded the letter carefully. "Same as my mum."

That afternoon, Graham's phone rang. He put it on speaker so Bev could hear. It was Simon Vance, from London.

"Graham." The voice was very different now. Warm, almost friendly. "Congratulations. The board has seen the subscription numbers. Very impressive."

"Thank you, Simon."

"The board has decided that the Ledger has a strong future. We'd like to invest in the newsroom. Two new investigative reporters. Maybe more."

Graham looked at Bev. Bev raised her eyebrows.

"And the advertising we lost?" asked Graham.

"Several new advertisers have contacted us. Apparently, companies want to be associated with a newspaper that people trust." Simon Vance paused. "Funny, isn't it?"

"Hilarious," said Graham, and hung up.

He sat in silence for a moment. Then he opened his drawer, took out an old bottle of whisky and five plastic cups.

"Just one," he said. "Then back to work."

Trois semaines après l'article, Graham a réuni tout le monde au milieu de la rédaction. Il tenait une feuille de papier, et son visage était encore plus rouge que d'habitude.

« Des chiffres, a-t-il dit. Depuis l'affaire Little Stars, six mille deux cents personnes ont pris un abonnement numérique au Ledger. Six mille deux cents. En trois semaines. C'est plus qu'en trois ans. »

Tout le monde a applaudi.

« Et ce n'est pas tout. » Graham a montré un coin de la salle. Il y avait quatre grands sacs postaux par terre. « Des lettres. De vraies lettres, sur papier, avec des timbres. Des gens qui nous remercient. Des gens qui nous racontent leur histoire. Des gens qui demandent comment aider St Anne's. »

Léa est allée vers les sacs et en a sorti quelques enveloppes. Un retraité de Bolton qui avait couru toutes les courses avec sa défunte épouse. Une classe d'école primaire de Stretford qui avait envoyé des dessins de l'hôpital avec « MERCI LE LEDGER » en grosses lettres de couleur.

Elle a trouvé Danny en train de lire une lettre dans un coin. Ses yeux étaient humides.

« D'une maman, a-t-il dit. Son fils était en cancérologie l'an dernier. Elle a dormi sur une chaise en plastique pendant quatre mois. » Il a plié la lettre avec soin. « Comme ma mère. »

Cet après-midi-là, le téléphone de Graham a sonné. Il l'a mis sur haut-parleur pour que Bev puisse entendre. C'était Simon Vance, de Londres.
